# The Funnel Home Page — Section by Section

> **Scope:** what goes on the page itself, top to bottom. Nothing about email
> sequences, tracking, or backend automation. Just: what sections exist, what
> order they go in, what belongs inside each one, and how to tell when a section
> is done.
>
> For the full funnel architecture around this page, see
> `funnel-layout-framework.md`.

---

## Before you place a single section

**The home page of a funnel is not a website home page.** There is no nav, no
"About," no blog link, no social icons, no second offer. It is one page with one
promise and one button repeated down the length of it.

Three rules govern every decision below:

1. **One page, one decision.** Name the single action before you design
   anything. Every section either moves the reader toward that action or it
   comes off the page.
2. **Sections go in objection order, not aesthetic order.** Each section
   answers one question the reader is silently asking, in the sequence they
   actually ask it. Answering out of order wastes the section.
3. **The page sells the free thing, not the paid thing.** If the price of your
   program appears anywhere on this page, you've skipped a step in the funnel.

### The objection sequence (this is the section order)

| The reader is thinking | The section that answers it |
|---|---|
| "Am I in the right place?" | 1 — Announcement bar |
| "What is this, and what does it cost me?" | 2 — Hero |
| "How do I get in?" | 3 — The form |
| "Why should I keep reading?" | 4 — Credibility strip |
| "Do you actually understand my situation?" | 5 — Is this you? |
| "What happens if I keep doing nothing?" | 6 — The stakes |
| "Is the outcome actually achievable?" | 7 — What you'll learn |
| "Wait — is this one of *those*?" | 8 — What this is not |
| "Who are you to teach this?" | 9 — Host |
| "Has it worked for someone like me?" | 10 — Proof |
| "What do I actually walk away with?" | 11 — What you get |
| "Why now instead of later?" | 12 — Urgency |
| "What's the catch?" | 13 — FAQ |
| "Okay — remind me what I'm signing up for." | 14 — Final CTA |
| — | 15 — Footer |

## Section 1 — Announcement bar

**Job:** categorize the page in under one second, before the headline is read.

**Contains:** one line, full width, highest-contrast band on the page. Three
facts only — that it's free, that it's live, and when.

> `FREE LIVE MASTERCLASS · THURSDAY 7PM ET · LIMITED SEATS`

**Rules**
- No button here. Framing only.
- No more than about 8 words. It's read peripherally, not deliberately.
- If the event is evergreen rather than live, say `INSTANT ACCESS` instead of
  faking a date. Do not invent a live date you can't honor.

**Done when:** a stranger glancing at the top 40px of the page can tell you what
kind of page this is and what it costs.

---

## Section 2 — Hero

**Job:** win the scroll. Most people who leave never see section 3, so this
section gets a disproportionate share of your time.

### Contents, in order

**Eyebrow** (2–5 words) — `FREE ONLINE MASTERCLASS`. Kills the "is this a sales
page?" flinch before the headline lands.

**Headline** (8–16 words). The reliable formula:

> How to **[specific outcome]** in **[timeframe]** — without **[the thing they
> dread or the obvious-but-wrong method]**

- The outcome must be **measurable**, not emotional. "Book 3 paid speaking gigs
  in 90 days" beats "become a confident speaker."
- The timeframe makes the claim concrete.
- The "without" is where your differentiation lives — it names your method by
  exclusion and quietly explains why their past attempts failed.

**Subhead** (1–2 sentences). Two jobs, both required:
- **Name the audience out loud** — "For coaches and consultants who…"
- **State what they leave with** — the tangible takeaway.

Naming the audience makes the wrong people leave. That is the point.

**Format line.** Day, date, time **with timezone**, duration, and the word
"live." State the duration explicitly — "90 minutes" — because the reader is
pricing the commitment in time, and a blank there gets filled in with "probably
forever."

**Primary button.** See section 3.

**Risk-reducer** — small type directly beneath the button:
> `100% free · 90 minutes · No experience required`

**Visual.** Either a photo of the host or a 30–60 second promo video.
- Real photo, not stock. Stock photography on a personal-brand funnel is a
  credibility leak.
- If the host is facing sideways, face them **toward** the button.
- Video: never autoplay with sound, always a visible play button, and the page
  must convert with the video ignored entirely.

### The fold test

On a phone, **headline + subhead + button must be visible without scrolling.**
If the visual pushes the button below the fold, shrink the visual. Check this on
an actual phone, not a browser resized to look like one.

**Done when:** someone who reads only this section knows what they'd get, who
it's for, when it happens, what it costs, and how to say yes.

## Section 3 — The opt-in form

**Job:** collect the registration with the least possible friction.

**Two patterns:**

- **Two-step (default for cold traffic).** The hero shows a button; clicking it
  opens a modal with the fields. The button click is a free micro-commitment,
  and people finish what they start.
- **Inline (better for warm traffic and retargeting).** Fields sit right in the
  hero for people who already know you and don't need convincing.

**Fields — every one costs conversion rate:**
- Name + Email — highest conversion
- Name + Email + Phone — the standard here, because text reminders are the
  biggest lever on whether anyone shows up. Worth the friction.
- Anything beyond that on a *free* registration is a mistake. Qualifying
  questions belong later, when the prospect is motivated enough to answer them.

**Button copy** — first person, outcome-flavored, never "Submit":
> `Save My Free Seat` · `Yes — Reserve My Spot` · `Send Me The Link`

**Consent.** If you collect a phone number, put plain-language SMS consent
directly beside the button.

**Done when:** the form asks for nothing you won't use in the next 72 hours.

## Section 4 — Credibility strip

**Job:** buy the next ten seconds of attention.

A thin band immediately below the fold. One of:
- Press or brand logos
- A hard number — `4,200+ students trained`, `$18M in client revenue`
- A single one-line marquee testimonial

**Rules**
- Only what is true and verifiable.
- Keep it visually quiet — grayscale logos, no heavy borders. This section
  supports; it doesn't perform.
- If you have neither logos nor numbers, skip it. An empty-feeling credibility
  strip is worse than none.

---

## Section 5 — "Is this you?"

**Job:** prove you understand their situation better than they can articulate
it themselves.

**Contains:** 3–5 bullets, present tense, first person, in *their* words.

Each bullet should be specific enough that the right reader thinks "how do they
know that?"

- Weak: "You struggle with visibility."
- Strong: "You've spoken for free four times this year and still haven't been
  paid once."

**Rules**
- Under 15 words per bullet. This block is scanned, not read.
- Describe **situations**, not feelings. "You're overwhelmed" is a mirror
  nobody recognizes themselves in; "your leads live in three different DM
  inboxes" is.
- Close with a bridge line: *"If you nodded at any of those, this masterclass
  was built for you."*

**Done when:** you could show these bullets to five people in your audience and
at least three would say "that's literally me."

---

## Section 6 — The stakes

**Job:** make doing nothing feel expensive.

Short — 2–4 sentences or three bullets. Name what another 6–12 months of the
current pattern actually costs: the money left on the table, the competitor who
moves first, the year that passes.

**Rules**
- Factual, not doom. One paragraph of consequence, then move on.
- Never fear-monger past the truth. Overcooking this reads as manipulation and
  costs you more trust than the urgency gains you.
- Optional section. Cut it if the pain block already lands hard.

---

## Section 7 — What you'll learn

**Job:** make the outcome feel achievable. This is the load-bearing section of
the page.

**Contains: exactly three items.** Three is substantial enough to feel worth an
hour and few enough to hold in memory.

Each follows this shape:

> **Secret #1: [curiosity-driving name]** — how to [outcome] without
> [objection], even if [their disqualifying belief].

The three should map to the three beliefs that have to break:

1. **The method works.**
2. **I personally can do this.**
3. **My circumstances won't stop me.**

That's the architecture, not a flourish. If your three items are just topics,
rewrite them until each one attacks one of those beliefs.

**Rules**
- **Name the outcome, don't teach the how.** Curiosity earns the registration;
  resolution is what the class delivers. A section that fully teaches the
  material removes the reason to attend.
- Whatever the three are here, they must be the same three taught in the class.
  This page is a table of contents.
- Put a **button at the end of this section.** It's the highest-intent scroll
  depth on the page.

**Done when:** a skeptic reading the three headings feels curious rather than
informed.

---

## Section 8 — What this is not

**Job:** disarm the "I've seen this before" reflex.

Short section. Three or four lines of the form:

> This is **not** a 90-minute infomercial.
> This is **not** for people looking for a get-rich-quick shortcut.
> This is **not** theory — you'll leave with [specific artifact].

**Rules**
- Naming the pitch honestly ("I'll make you an offer at the end, and you're
  free to ignore it") tends to *raise* both registrations and show-up quality.
  Sophisticated audiences already assume the pitch; saying it first converts
  suspicion into trust.
- Use this to exclude, too. Repelling bad-fit registrants is worth more than
  the volume.

---

## Section 9 — Host

**Job:** transfer authority.

A résumé does not convert. A transformation story does. Four beats:

1. **Where I was** — the specific, unflattering low point.
2. **What changed** — the discovery, in one line.
3. **Where I am now** — proof, with numbers.
4. **Why I teach this** — the reason it isn't just about money.

**Rules**
- First person. 4–6 short paragraphs.
- One real photo.
- Credentials go in a supporting line at the end, never as the lead.
- The low point should be genuinely uncomfortable to publish. That discomfort is
  what makes it land.

---

## Section 10 — Proof

**Job:** answer "has this worked for someone like me?"

3–6 testimonials, in descending order of power:

1. Video (30–60 seconds)
2. Screenshots of real messages, DMs, or dashboards
3. Photo + full name + specific numeric result
4. Text + first name only ← weak; treat as filler

**Rules**
- Every testimonial needs a **number or a before/after state.** "Changed my
  life" is decoration; "went from $0 to three $5k bookings in 11 weeks" is
  evidence.
- Choose people who match your audience's **starting point**, not your most
  impressive outcome ever. The reader is looking for someone like them, not
  someone above them.
- If results are shown, an earnings disclaimer belongs in the footer.
- Put a **button at the end of this section.**

---

## Section 11 — What you get

**Job:** make a free event feel like it has weight.

List the tangible takeaways — the workbook, the template, the swipe file, the
scorecard, the recording. Three to five items, each one line.

**Rules**
- Only list things that actually exist and will actually be delivered.
- Assigning dollar values to free bonuses is a dated tactic that reads as
  cheap to most audiences now. Describe the artifact instead: *"the exact
  one-page outline I use to structure a paid talk."*
- Optional section — but it's often the difference between "a webinar" and
  "worth blocking 90 minutes for."

---

## Section 12 — Urgency

**Job:** answer "why now?"

**Only use urgency that is real.** Countdown timers that reset on refresh are a
trust risk and an ad-policy risk, and audiences recognize them instantly.

Legitimate mechanisms:
- **The date itself.** The class is Thursday. Thursday is the timer.
- **A genuine seat cap** tied to your platform's actual limit.
- **A registration cutoff** — "doors close one hour before we start."

If nothing is genuinely scarce, use the date and skip the timer. A calendar date
is honest urgency and works fine.

---

## Section 13 — FAQ

**Job:** clear the last few blockers for the minority who need it.

4–6 items, in an accordion so it stays short for everyone else. Answer the real
objections, not softballs. For this page type, the standing four are almost
always:

- **"Is it really free?"** — Yes, and say what you'll offer at the end.
- **"Will there be a replay?"** — Answer strategically. "Replay for live
  attendees only" protects live attendance, which is where conversion happens.
- **"How long is it?"** — Exact duration.
- **"Is this for me if I'm brand new / already experienced?"** — Reassure the
  segment you want; gently exclude the one you don't.

**Rules**
- If you find yourself writing a soft question with a flattering answer, cut it.
  A fake FAQ signals that the real objections weren't addressed.

---

## Section 14 — Final CTA

**Job:** convert the skimmers who scrolled straight to the bottom.

Contains, in one compact block:
- The headline promise restated in a single line
- The format line again — free, date, time, duration
- The button
- One line of risk-reducer copy

**Never end a page on the FAQ.** The last thing on the page before the legal
footer should always be the ask.

---

## Section 15 — Footer

**Job:** be legally complete and offer zero exits.

- Copyright and business entity name
- Privacy Policy, Terms, and SMS terms if collecting phone numbers
- **Earnings/results disclaimer** if any outcome or income is implied anywhere
  above
- **Platform disclaimer:** "This site is not part of, or endorsed by, Meta /
  Facebook / Instagram."
- **No social icons. No outbound links** other than the legal pages. A social
  icon in the footer is an exit ramp you paid for.

## Layout mechanics across all sections

**Button placement.** Minimum three buttons on the page; five is normal. Roughly
one every 1.5 screen-heights. **Every button on the page does the same thing** —
one offer, one action, one destination. A secondary "learn more" button splits
intent and lowers total conversions.

**Sticky mobile bar.** A fixed bottom CTA bar that appears once the hero scrolls
out of view. On mobile-heavy paid traffic this is one of the highest-return
single elements on the page.

**Color.** One accent color, used **only** on buttons. The moment your button
color appears in a heading or an icon, it stops reading as "clickable."

**Section rhythm.** Alternate background tones — white / off-white / white —
to create section boundaries without drawing lines everywhere. Use a single
dark or saturated section for emphasis, usually section 7.

**Spacing.** 64–96px vertical padding between sections on desktop, 40–56px on
mobile. Cramped sections read as cluttered regardless of how good the copy is.

**Width.** Content max ~1100–1200px; body text columns 600–700px. Full-width
paragraphs are unreadable on a laptop.

**Type.** Mobile headline 28–34px, section headings 22–26px, body 16–18px.
Nothing below 14px except legal copy.

**Length.** Four to eight screens is normal for a free offer on cold traffic.
Longer isn't better — but neither is shorter. The page ends when the objections
are answered, and the objections are the ones listed in the table at the top of
this document.

**Mobile first.** Design the phone layout first and adapt upward. Tap targets at
least 44px tall, full-width buttons, no horizontal scroll anywhere.

## If you only build six sections

For a fast launch, build these and add the rest later:

1. Hero (headline, subhead, format line, button)
2. Is this you?
3. What you'll learn — the three
4. Host
5. Proof
6. Final CTA + footer

That's a complete argument. Everything else on this page strengthens it.

---

## What never goes on this page

- Navigation menu
- Links to your main website, blog, store, or podcast
- Social media icons
- A second offer, a second button destination, or a "book a call" alternative
- The price of your paid program
- Stock photography of generic people in an office
- A fake countdown timer
- An autoplaying video with sound
